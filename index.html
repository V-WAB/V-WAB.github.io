Where we stand

The shelf we intend to sit on.

Two questions decide this category. Does it come from where it says it comes from, and does it feel worth the money. Most shea sits low on one axis or the other. We are building for the corner where both hold.

The story

My grandmother never called it skincare.

She called it butter, and she made it in the yard in Tamale on a Saturday. Nuts roasted until the smell filled the compound, kernels pounded, paste worked by hand in a calabash of cold water until it turned pale and light. I was small enough to be in the way, so my job was to fetch water and watch. I watched for years.

What she made was better than most of what I later saw sold in glass. The gap was never the butter. It was everything around the butter, the consistency, the packaging, the proof, the reason a stranger in London or Lagos should believe a jar from northern Ghana is worth what it is worth.

So Banini is not an attempt to invent something. It is her method, held to a standard that travels. We whip in small batches, we test with real people before we sell, and we say plainly where the nuts come from. I want a Ghanaian shea butter that can sit on the same shelf as L'Occitane without pretending, even slightly, to be from anywhere else.

Nurtured by nature, finished by hand.Founder, Banini Butter, Tamale

The jar

Four things in, nothing else.

Every jar is whipped, never melted and poured. Melting collapses the crystal structure and gives you the greasy film most people think shea has to leave. Whipping keeps it light, so it sinks in and stays put.

  • Base

    Unrefined shea butterNorthern Region, Ghana, gathered and processed by women's cooperatives near Tamale.

  • Oil

    Cold-pressed baobab oilPressed without heat, so the vitamins survive the journey into the jar.

  • Method

    Whipped, not meltedHand-churned in small batches until the texture holds a soft peak.

  • Never

    No parabens, no fillers, no waterNothing added to make the jar heavier or the margin easier.
